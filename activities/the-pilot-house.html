Part 1 — Name the tool

A writer can tell you “Brown was mean.” A good writer makes you feel it instead, using three kinds of tools:

The tools

Simile and metaphor are both figurative language — saying something by comparing it to something else.

Watch it work first

Plain: “The horse was mad at me.”

With tools: “She pinned her ears flat and swung her back end at me like a slammed gate.”

Tools: imagery (you can see the ears and the swing) and a simile (“like a slammed gate”).
What it does to you: you see the danger instead of being told about it.
What it sets up: you already know a kick is coming.

Now Twain. Read each line (out loud helps), then tap the tool doing the most work. One line is arguable — if you can back it, you're right.

Part 2 — What it does to you

Naming the tool is only half the answer. The other half is what it does to you while you read — what you feel, what you see, what you start to believe about Brown. That is how word choice builds meaning.

Pick what each line does to you. Three of the choices belong to none of the lines.

Part 3 — What it sets up

Structure is how a piece is built: what comes first, what builds, where it blows up. Twain’s chapter is built like pressure in a pipe.

Tap the job each line does in the whole piece.

Part 4 — Your turn

Now you be Twain. Pick someone — or something — that makes life hard: a boss in a game, a horse having a bad day, a strict coach, a little brother. Write about them so the reader feels it, without ever saying “they were awful.”
