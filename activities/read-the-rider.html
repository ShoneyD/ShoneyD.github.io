Read the Rider

Wren has ridden horses since she was six. Biscuit is a rescue horse who doesn’t trust anybody yet. Here are five things Wren says over one week at the barn, in order. She never once tells you who she is. Her dialogue does.

Part 1 — Hear her voice

Dialogue is what characters say out loud. Voice is how they say it: the words they pick, how long the sentences run, the punctuation. Read each line out loud, then tap the voice you hear. You can change your mind before you press Check.

Done looks like: all five lines tapped, then Check.

The four voices

Part 2 — What she wants

A character’s motivation is what they want. Wren never says “I want…” in any of these lines. Her words give it away anyway: who she’s talking to, and what she’s trying to get out of them.

Pick what she wants in each line. Three of the choices belong to none of the lines. They’re close, so read carefully.

Done looks like: five picks, then Check.

Part 3 — Who she is right now

A character trait is what kind of person someone is. Answer for this line, this moment only. Watch what happens to your answers as you go down the page: when a character’s traits change over a story, that’s character development.

One trait fits none of the lines. One line is genuinely arguable. If you can back it, you’re right.

Part 4 — Your turn

Put the five lines side by side and Wren is a different person at the end than at the start. Your job: find where she turns, prove the change, then write her new voice yourself.

Done looks like: one pick, two sentences, one line of dialogue. Spelling doesn’t count here.

Starters, only if you want one

• At the start, Wren sounds ____ when she says “____.” By the end she sounds ____ when she says “____,” which shows she has become ____.

Use one, or ignore both and write it your way.

Next

Use the same three questions on any story you read: How do they talk? What do they want? Who are they right now? Ask them at the start and the end, and the difference is the character’s development.
