      Introduce the Concept

      Part 1 — Says vs. sounds

      Quick refresher. A man tells us how he killed the old man he lived with — and he insists, again and again, that he is not mad. He hid the body under the floorboards. When the police came, he was calm. Then he heard a heartbeat that nobody else could hear, and he confessed.

      A character's motivation is why they do what they do. Sometimes the character tells you. Here's the trick: don't believe them yet. Listen to how they sound — that's tone, the attitude in the words.

      Here's how it works on something you know. A rider says she's skipping Saturday's show because her horse "has been a little off this week." But she says it fast, sounds relieved, and changes the subject.

      Part 2 — From motive to theme

      A theme is what the story is saying about people in general — something that would still be true about anyone who did what he did. The motive is about one man. The theme is about everybody.

      Here's the end of the story. The police have searched and found nothing. They're sitting, chatting, smiling. He can hear a heartbeat getting louder.

      Q4. Why does he confess? This one has two good answers — pick the one you can back up.

      My answer

      Check my answer — after you've written yours

      Not the first one — the police found nothing and were happy to chat. Either of the other two works if you can point at a word: \"I must scream or die\" backs guilt; \"derision\" and \"hypocritical smiles\" back the feeling of being watched and judged.

      Passage A · Edgar Allan Poe, “The Cask of Amontillado,” 1846

      Montresor, the narrator, is talking about a man named Fortunato.

      First: borne = put up with · ventured upon = dared · vowed = swore · impunity = getting away with it, no punishment

      The thousand injuries of Fortunato I had borne as I best could, but when he ventured upon insult I vowed revenge.

      I must not only punish but punish with impunity.

      He had a weak point—this Fortunato—although in other regards he was a man to be respected and even feared.

      Passage B · O. Henry, “The Gift of the Magi,” 1905

      Della has very little money and wants to buy her husband, Jim, a Christmas present.

      First: shabby = old and worn out · sterling = top quality, the real thing · worthy = good enough

      One dollar and eighty-seven cents. That was all. And sixty cents of it was in pennies.

      Three times Della counted it. One dollar and eighty-seven cents. And the next day would be Christmas.

      There was clearly nothing to do but flop down on the shabby little couch and howl. So Della did it.

      Many a happy hour she had spent planning for something nice for him. Something fine and rare and sterling—something just a little bit near to being worthy of the honor of being owned by Jim.

      Tone, motivation, theme — and the tell — open this any time
      ToneThe attitude in the words — how a character sounds, not what they say. Proud, defensive, panicked, sweet, cold…
      MotivationWhy a character does what they do. Not always the reason they give.
      ThemeWhat the story is saying about people in general — something that would still be true about anyone in the same spot.
      The tellWhen what a character says and how they sound don't match, the tone is usually the one telling the truth.

      The question that does the work: what do they say they want — and what do they sound like they want?
