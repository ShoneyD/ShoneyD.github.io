      Introduce the Concept

      Part 1 — The three controls · about 6 min

      A writer trying to move you is flying a plane, and you are the plane. They only have three controls. That's it. Three.

      TRUST

      ethos

      The writer shows you why they're worth listening to.

      In the air

      Before you board, you want to know the pilot has 12,000 hours — not 12. Ethos is a writer showing you their hours.

      FEELING

      pathos

      The writer makes you feel something — sad, angry, scared, hopeful.

      In the air

      That drop in your stomach when the plane hits rough air. Nobody had to explain it to you. Pathos gets there the same way.

      FACTS

      logos

      The writer gives you numbers and reasons.

      In the air

      The instrument panel. Altitude, fuel, speed. It doesn't care how you feel — it just tells you where you are.

      Q1. Here are three ways somebody could try to talk you into starting a business. Which control is each one flying with?

      A — "I built and sold two companies before I was thirty."

      B — "Nine out of ten people who start one are still running it five years later."

      C — "Imagine never having to ask anyone for permission again."

      Done looks like

      All three dropdowns have something picked. Then we'll talk about them.

      Part 2 — Impact: the three-step chain · about 5 min

      Naming the control is the easy half. Anybody can point at a gauge. Impact means saying where the plane actually went.

      Every single time, in this order:

      1NAME IT

      Which control is this? Trust, Feeling, or Facts?

      ↓
      2FEEL IT

      What does it do to the reader? Say it as a feeling or a thought the reader now has.

      ↓
      3WHY IT WORKS

      Why did the writer put it right there? What would be missing without it?

      Here's one done for you

      The line: "I built and sold two companies before I was thirty."

      1. NAME IT — TRUST (ethos).

      2. FEEL IT — The reader stops arguing in their head. They think: okay, this person has actually done it, so maybe I should listen.

      3. WHY IT WORKS — He says it first, before any advice. If he gave the advice first, the reader would spend the whole time wondering who he is. Getting trust out of the way early buys him the rest of the page.

      Q2. Your turn, on the other one. The line: "Imagine never having to ask anyone for permission again."

      1. Name it

      2. Feel it — what does it do to the reader?

      3. Why it works — why put it there?

      Sentence starters — use them, that's what they're for

      • It makes the reader feel ____.
      • It makes the reader picture themselves ____.
      • He puts it there because ____.
      • Without it, the reader would ____.

      One or two sentences is plenty. Don't worry about spelling — and you can say it out loud and I'll type it.

      Done looks like

      One control picked, and two short answers — one about the reader, one about the writer's choice.

      Part 3 — Five words before we read · about 2 min

      These five turn up in the article, or in what we say about it. Read them once. You don't have to memorise anything — this page stays open.

      Take these with you

      anecdotea short true story about something that happened to a real person
      credibilitya reason to believe somebody knows what they're talking about
      prevalentvery common — happening a lot, to a lot of people
      insidiouslooks harmless, but is actually dangerous
      erodeto wear away slowly — the way wind wears down the edge of a runway

      Independent Mastery

      Part 1 — Fly it solo · about 7 min

      Two lines from the very end of the article — the part we never talked about. No hints on these ones. That's the point: this is where you find out you've got it.

      You can type these or say them out loud and I'll type — your call, and don't worry about spelling.

      Line A

      "Our need for human connection is like our need for food and water: essential for our survival."

      1. Name it

      2. Feel it — what does it do to the reader?

      3. Why it works — why is this line in the last paragraph and not the first?

      Line B

      "This work will take all of us: schools, workplaces, community organizations, government, health workers, public health professionals, individuals, families and more working together."

      1. Name it

      2. He could have written "everyone." He wrote out the whole list instead. What does the long list do to the reader — and why did he do it?

      Homework

      Homework — Spot it in the wild

      Rhetoric isn't a school thing. It's running at you all day. Find one moment of it and run the three steps on it.

      Pick whichever you'd rather — or pick your own and we'll use that.

      A. A video or post telling you how to make money.
      B. A pilot's announcement, or an airline safety video.
      C. Any advert that ran before something you watched.

      Q1. Which one did you pick, and what exactly did they say? (Write down the words, or as close as you can get.)

      Q2. Name it — which control?

      Q3. Feel it, and why it works — what did it do to you, and why do you think they said it that way?

      Sentence starters — use them, that's what they're for

      • They said ____.
      • It made me ____.
      • They said it that way because they wanted me to ____.

      Bullet points are fine. Don't worry about spelling.
