      Independent Mastery

      Part 1 — The woman in the wallpaper

      You've already met Poe's madman in "The Tell-Tale Heart". Here is his cousin, written while the first Sherlock Holmes stories were filling the magazines. Nobody is murdered. Something worse happens.

      What happens. Charlotte Perkins Gilman, "The Yellow Wallpaper" (1892). A young mother is told she is "nervous" and needs total rest. Her husband John, a doctor, rents an old mansion and keeps her in an upstairs room with barred windows and ugly yellow wallpaper. She isn't allowed to write, so she keeps a secret journal. Day after day she stares at the wallpaper, and she starts to see a woman trapped behind the pattern, creeping.

      Listen for: who she thinks is the problem, and who you think is.

      John laughs at me, of course, but one expects that in marriage.
      You see, he does not believe I am sick!
      I've got out at last, … And I've pulled off most of the paper, so you can't put me back!
      Stuck? Tap for plain English (read the real lines first)

      1: My husband laughs at me, but husbands do that. 2: He doesn't think anything is really wrong with me. 3 (the ending): She now believes SHE is the woman from the wallpaper. She has torn the paper down so no one can "put her back".

      Two ways the Gothic grew up — open this any time
      The Gothic toolboxA curse, a fog-covered moor, an old hall, a candle in a window, something that might be supernatural.
      The detective storySame toolbox, plus a detective who uses clues and reason to explain the 'supernatural' away. The monster turns out to be a person.
      Red herringA clue that looks guilty and isn't. It keeps you guessing.
      The psychological thrillerNo detective needed. The story lives inside one mind, often an unreliable narrator. The monster is inside.
      Unreliable narratorThe person telling the story can't be trusted, and often doesn't know it. The horror is the gap between what he says and what we see.
