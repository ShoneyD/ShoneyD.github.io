      Introduce the Concept

      Part 1 — The dead travel fast

      In the first chapter of Dracula (1897), Jonathan Harker is riding a coach toward the Count's castle. He hears the other passengers whisper a line of poetry:

      Denn die Todten reiten schnell
      “For the dead travel fast”

      That line was already 124 years old when Stoker used it. It comes from a German poem called “Lenore”, written in 1773. Every Gothic story you like is a remix. Today you meet the originals.

      Part 2 — Lenore: the dead rider

      What happens. Germany, 1773. Lenore's fiancé went to war and never came home. She is so angry that she curses God. At midnight a rider who looks like her fiancé knocks, and she climbs up behind him. They ride faster and faster through the dark. At sunrise he turns out to be Death, and the “wedding bed” he promised her is a grave.

      Listen for: one line that keeps coming back.

      When she asks why they're riding so fast, he answers with the line you already know:

      The dead travel fast.

      Part 3 — The Rime: the cursed sailor

      Three old words first. You'll need them at the end of this part:

      dothdoes
      treadto step, to walk
      fienda demon, an evil spirit

      What happens. England, 1798. An old sailor stops a stranger on his way to a wedding and won't let him leave until he hears a story. Years ago, out at sea, the sailor shot an albatross for no reason. The crew believed the bird was good luck. Then the ship is cursed: no wind, no water, every other sailor drops dead, and he is left alone with the bodies.

      Listen for: a word that he can't stop saying.

      With my cross-bow
      I shot the ALBATROSS.
      Alone, alone, all, all alone,
      Alone on a wide wide sea!

      Q3. He grabs a stranger and won't let go until the story is told. What does that tell you about the person telling it?

      My answer

      This one has two good answers. Pick one and back it out loud. Late in the poem he describes how he feels, even after the curse lifts:

      Like one, that on a lonesome road
      Doth walk in fear and dread,
      And having once turned round walks on,
      And turns no more his head;
      Because he knows, a frightful fiend
      Doth close behind him tread.

      He's like someone walking alone down an empty road, scared. He looked back once, and now he won't turn his head again, because he knows a terrible demon is walking right behind him.

      Part 1 — A killer with good manners

      Robert Browning, “Porphyria's Lover” (1836). Victorian. Three words first:

      sullenmoody, sulking
      vexto annoy, to stir up
      Porphyriaher name (por-FEER-ee-uh)

      What happens. A stormy night. A man sits alone in a cottage. Porphyria, a woman from a richer family, comes in out of the rain, lights the fire, and shows him she loves him. He decides this perfect moment has to last forever, so he strangles her with her own hair. Then he sits with her body all night, calm and pleased with himself. The whole poem is him telling it.

      Listen for: how calm he sounds.

      The rain set early in to-night,
      The sullen wind was soon awake,
      and all her hair
      In one long yellow string I wound
      Three times her little throat around,
      And strangled her. No pain felt she;
      I am quite sure she felt no pain.
      Stuck? Tap for plain English (read the real lines first)

      He took her long blonde hair, wound it three times around her neck, and strangled her. Then he tells us he's sure it didn't hurt her.

      Independent Mastery

      Part 1 — Come buy

      Christina Rossetti, “Goblin Market” (1862). Victorian. A poem you haven't seen.

      What happens. Two sisters, Laura and Lizzie. Every morning and evening, goblin men by the stream call out, selling magical fruit. Laura buys some, paying with a lock of her golden hair, and eats until she can't stop. After that she can't hear the goblins any more, can't get more fruit, and starts wasting away. Lizzie goes to the goblins herself to save her.

      Listen for: what the goblins say, over and over.

      “Come buy our orchard fruits,
      Come buy, come buy:
      We must not look at goblin men,
      We must not buy their fruits:
      Who knows upon what soil they fed
      Their hungry thirsty roots?
      Her hair grew thin and grey;

      The three questions, and where the monster lives — open this any time
      1 · Who's telling it?The speaker. Can you trust them? Calm, scared, haunted, lying to themselves?
      2 · What's the sound doing?Repeated words, rhythm, rhyme. What does it make you feel: speed, panic, a lullaby, a sales pitch?
      3 · Where's the monster?OUTSIDE: a ghost, a curse, something that comes for you. INSIDE: a mind, a hunger, a person who looks normal.
      The roots (1773 and 1798)Bürger's Lenore and Coleridge's Rime of the Ancient Mariner. Folk-ballad sound, the supernatural, the monster mostly comes from outside.
      The Victorian branch (1830s to 1890s)Browning, Rossetti, then Jekyll and Hyde and Dracula. The monster moves inside: into a voice, a craving, a respectable person.
