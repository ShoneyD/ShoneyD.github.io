      Introduce the Concept

      Part 1 — The complexity dial

      Writers choose how hard their language is to read. That setting is a choice, and it changes how the words land.

      Bus A: The bus was late. Again. I waited in the rain.

      Bus B: Having waited, once more, beneath a sky that seemed to take personal pleasure in my misery, I began to suspect that the bus, like all of my hopes, had simply decided not to come.

      Q1. Which version sounds like the writer is being dramatic, even a little funny, about it?

      Q12. Compare the two writers in two sentences. Use your Pillow Book line (Q6) and your 1984 line (Q11).

      Sentence starters — use them if you want

      • Sei Shōnagon's words make her world feel … , but Orwell's words make his world feel … because ____.

      You don't have to use these.

      My answer

      This one's meant to be hard. Both writers use a list. Sei Shōnagon's whole piece is lists. Orwell has a famous three-part list too: the Party's slogans on the Ministry of Truth, in chapter 1. Find them in your copy.

      Q13. Same tool, opposite job. What does the Party's list do that Sei Shōnagon's lists don't?

      Sentence starters — use them if you want

      • Her lists feel … but the Party's list feels … , because ____.

      You don't have to use these.

      My answer

      Check my answer — after you've written yours

      One strong reading: her lists are one real person telling you her honest opinions, so they feel personal and alive. The Party's list has no person behind it. It uses short, plain words to make things that can't be true sound like simple facts, and it's built to be repeated, not thought about. Same tool, but hers opens up a mind and theirs shuts one down. If you got anything like 'hers is personal, theirs is control,' you've got it.

      Words for how writing works — open this any time
      Language complexityHow much work the reader has to do: sentence length, how formal or rare the words are, how much you hold in your head at once.
      SyntaxHow sentences are built. Short and choppy, long and winding, a list, a fragment.
      DictionWord choice. Why this word and not a plainer one?
      Figurative languageSaying something that isn't literally true to make a point: comparison, exaggeration, a strong image.
      IronyThe words say one thing, the writer means the opposite, and expects you to notice.
      Comparing choicesTwo writers, a similar job, different tools. What does each choice change for the reader?

      Every answer needs a because. Naming the tool is half. Saying what it does to the reader is the other half.
