      Introduce the Concept

      Part 1 — Who hears what

      In a novel, the writer can just tell you what a character is thinking. In a play, all you get is people talking. So playwrights use tricks to let you hear what a character really thinks.

      Guided Practice

      Part 1 — The road to the dagger

      Who's who

      Moment 1. Act I, scene 3. Three witches greet Macbeth as "Thane of Cawdor" (a lord's title) and say he'll be king. Minutes later, a messenger tells him the king has just made him Thane of Cawdor. One prediction has come true. He turns away from his friends and says this as an aside:

      This supernatural soliciting Cannot be ill, cannot be good. … … why do I yield to that suggestion Whose horrid image doth unfix my hair And make my seated heart knock at my ribs …?

      (unfix my hair = make my hair stand on end)

      Q4. What is Macbeth already picturing, the one that makes his hair stand on end?

      My answer

      Moment 2. Act I, scene 4. King Duncan has just executed the old Thane of Cawdor for being a traitor. He says:

      He was a gentleman on whom I built An absolute trust.

      Right as he says it, Macbeth walks in, and Duncan greets him warmly and says he'll stay the night at Macbeth's castle.

      Q5. Why is that moment dramatic irony?

      My answer

      Moment 3. Act I, scene 5. Lady Macbeth reads his letter about the witches. She decides Duncan must die tonight, but she worries about her husband:

      Yet do I fear thy nature; It is too full o' th' milk of human kindness To catch the nearest way.

      Moment 4. Act I, scene 7. Duncan is asleep in their castle. Macbeth, alone, in a soliloquy, lists every reason NOT to kill him, then admits:

      I have no spur To prick the sides of my intent, but only Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself And falls on th' other.

      (spur = the spike a rider kicks a horse with to make it go)

      He tells his wife he's out. She answers with a monologue that ends:

      When you durst do it, then you were a man … … screw your courage to the sticking place, And we'll not fail.

      Q8. Two sentences: how has Macbeth changed from Moment 1 to Moment 4, and what pushed him?

      Sentence starters — use them if you want

      • In Moment 1 Macbeth … , but by Moment 4 he … because ____.

      You don't have to use these.

      My answer

      Check my answer — after you've written yours

      One strong reading: in Moment 1 the idea of murder scares him so much his hair stands up. By Moment 4 he admits ambition is his only reason and even tries to back out, but Lady Macbeth attacks his pride and he agrees. He changes from scared of the thought to ready to do it. If yours names a change AND what caused it, you've got it.

      Part 2 — Cracking Shakespeare's words

      You don't need a dictionary for most of Shakespeare. You need two tools:

      Watch it once: supernatural soliciting. Root: super = above (superhero, superior). Clue: he's talking about witches. So supernatural = above or beyond nature: magic. And soliciting? Clue: the witches are pushing an idea at him. It means urging, tempting.

      Independent Mastery

      Part 1 — Blood that won't wash off

      Moment 5. Act II, scene 1. Midnight. Macbeth is alone, on his way to Duncan's room. He sees a dagger floating in the air in front of him:

      Is this a dagger which I see before me, The handle toward my hand? Come, let me clutch thee. I have thee not, and yet I see thee still. … … or art thou but A dagger of the mind, a false creation, Proceeding from the heat-oppressèd brain?

      A bell rings. It's Lady Macbeth's signal. His last lines:

      I go, and it is done. The bell invites me. Hear it not, Duncan, for it is a knell That summons thee to heaven or to hell.

      My answer

      Q13. The floating dagger is most likely…

      My answer

      Q14. "Hear it not, Duncan, for it is a knell / That summons thee to heaven or to hell." From the clues, a knell is…

      My answer

      Moment 6. Act II, scene 2. It's done. Macbeth comes back with Duncan's blood on his hands. He stares at them:

      Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood Clean from my hand? No, this my hand will rather The multitudinous seas incarnadine, Making the green one red.

      (Neptune = the Roman god of the sea)

      Lady Macbeth has blood on her hands too. She says:

      A little water clears us of this deed.

      Q15. Two sentences: how are Macbeth and Lady Macbeth different right after the murder, and what does that show about each of them?

      Sentence starters — use them if you want

      You don't have to use these.

      My answer

      This one's meant to be hard. Two words in his line look impossible. Use the roots.

      Q16. Use the roots and the clue "Making the green one red" to explain his line. What does he say his hand will do to the seas, and what does that tell you about his guilt?

      Sentence starters — use them if you want

      • Multitudinous means … and incarnadine means … , so he's saying … , which shows his guilt is ____.

      You don't have to use these.

      My answer

      Check my answer — after you've written yours

      Multitudinous = many, countless. Incarnadine = turn flesh- or blood-red. So instead of the ocean washing his hand clean, his bloody hand would turn all the oceans of the world red. He feels his guilt is too big to ever wash away, while Lady Macbeth thinks a little water will do it. Keep that difference in mind: the play has more to say about which of them is right.

      Play words and word tricks — open this any time
      AsideOne quick line to the audience. The other characters on stage can't hear it.
      SoliloquyA character alone on stage, thinking out loud. The truest look inside their head.
      MonologueA long speech TO another character.
      Dramatic ironyThe audience knows something a character doesn't.
      MotiveWHY a character does something: what they want, or what they're afraid of.
      Character developmentHow a character changes from the start to the end.
      Context clueThe words around a hard word that hint at what it means.
      RootA word part with its own meaning: super = above, multi = many, carn = flesh.

      Shakespeare's English: thou / thee = you, art = are, doth = does, hath = has, 'tis = it is.
