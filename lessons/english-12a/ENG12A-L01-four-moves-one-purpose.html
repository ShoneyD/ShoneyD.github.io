      Introduce the Concept

      Part 1 — About it, or aimed at it

      Do these two parts in whatever order you want.

      Nonfiction is true. That does not make it neutral. Every essay, speech and memoir is aimed at something, and the writer chooses every word to get it there.

      Here is the thing worth holding onto: what a piece of nonfiction is about is almost never what it is aimed at.

      Zora Neale Hurston's essay is about being thirteen and moving away from the all-Black town she grew up in. It is aimed at who gets to decide what you are. Look at how much of that she does in nine words:

      “I remember the very day that I became colored.”
      Zora Neale Hurston, “How It Feels to Be Colored Me,” 1928

      Q1. One word in that sentence is doing almost all of the work. Which one, and what does it do that a different word wouldn't?

      My answer

      Starters — only if you want one

      • The word ____ is doing the work, because it makes it sound like ____ rather than ____.
      • If she had written ____ instead, the sentence would ____.

      You don't have to use these — your own sentence is better if you've got one.

      Done looks like

      One word named, and one sentence saying what it does. Not what it means — what it does.

      Part 2 — Swap the word, break the sentence

      Here is the test that works on any nonfiction sentence anywhere: find the loaded word and swap it for its neutral twin. If the sentence changes sides, you've found where the argument was hiding.

      What the writer wrote Neutral twin What got lost
      the dirty work of Empire the daily work of Empire Orwell's entire opinion. He never states it — “dirty” states it for him.
      I became colored I was colored The date. The event. The fact that somebody else did it to her.

      Now try it on a sentence that has been aimed at an entire hall of people:

      “What, to the American slave, is your 4th of July?”
      Frederick Douglass, speaking in Rochester, 5 July 1852

      Q2. Rewrite Douglass's line without the word your. Then say, in one sentence, what the line lost.

      My rewrite, then what it lost

      Q3. Pick either Orwell's line or Hurston's line. Say what the essay it comes from is probably aimed at — not what it's about — and quote the words that got you there.

      Pick whichever you'd rather — or name a bigger idea neither of us has said yet, and we'll use that one.

      Done looks like

      A rewritten Douglass line and one sentence on what it lost. Then a bigger idea in your own words, with at least two words quoted from the line that led you to it.

      The four moves — open this any time
      ▤The Pile-Up — stacks example on example and refuses to stop. The length is the point.
      ◆The Loaded Word — one ordinary-looking word smuggles in a judgment. Swap it for its neutral twin and the sentence changes sides.
      ?The Question That Isn't a Question — asked so you feel the answer, not so anyone answers it.
      ⧉The Two Pictures — sets the version you expected beside the version that's true, and lets the gap do the arguing.

      Nobody is going to ask you for the technical names. You are going to be asked what the move does.
