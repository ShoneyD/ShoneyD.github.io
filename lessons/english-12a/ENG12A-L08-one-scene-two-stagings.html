      Introduce the Concept

      Part 1 — What actors add

      A script only gives you the words. Everything else, the actors and the director decide. That's called the staging.

      Guided Practice

      Part 1 — The sleepwalking scene

      What happens in Acts III and IV (Moments 1–6 were last lesson):

      Act V, scene 1. Night, in Macbeth's castle. A doctor and a gentlewoman (a lady's servant) are waiting up to watch Lady Macbeth. Lightly trimmed: the doctor's first few lines are cut.

      Q4. How do you want to read it?

      My answer

      Gentlewoman: Lo you, here she comes! This is her very guise; and, upon my life, fast asleep. Observe her; stand close.

      Doctor: How came she by that light?

      Gentlewoman: Why, it stood by her: she has light by her continually; 'tis her command.

      Doctor: You see, her eyes are open.

      Gentlewoman: Ay, but their sense is shut.

      Doctor: What is it she does now? Look, how she rubs her hands.

      Gentlewoman: It is an accustomed action with her, to seem thus washing her hands: I have known her continue in this a quarter of an hour.

      Lady Macbeth: Yet here's a spot.

      Doctor: Hark! she speaks.

      Lady Macbeth: Out, damned spot! out, I say! One: two: why, then, 'tis time to do't. Hell is murky! Fie, my lord, fie! a soldier, and afeard? What need we fear who knows it, when none can call our power to account? Yet who would have thought the old man to have had so much blood in him.

      Doctor: Do you mark that?

      Lady Macbeth: The thane of Fife had a wife: where is she now? What, will these hands ne'er be clean? No more o' that, my lord, no more o' that: you mar all with this starting.

      Doctor: Go to, go to; you have known what you should not.

      Lady Macbeth: Here's the smell of the blood still: all the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand. Oh, oh, oh!

      Doctor: What a sigh is there! The heart is sorely charged.

      Lady Macbeth: Wash your hands, put on your nightgown; look not so pale. I tell you yet again, Banquo's buried; he cannot come out on's grave.

      Lady Macbeth: To bed, to bed! there's knocking at the gate: come, come, come, come, give me your hand. What's done cannot be undone. To bed, to bed, to bed!

      Doctor: Unnatural deeds do breed unnatural troubles: infected minds to their deaf pillows will discharge their secrets. More needs she the divine than the physician.

      (afeard = afraid · the thane of Fife = Macduff · you mar all with this starting = you ruin everything by flinching · the divine = a priest · the physician = a doctor)

      My answer

      This one's meant to be hard. Back in Act III, Lady Macbeth told her husband to stop worrying about what they'd done: "Things without all remedy / Should be without regard: what's done is done." Now, asleep, she says: "What's done cannot be undone."

      Q13. Almost the same words. What has changed in what they mean, and what does that show about her?

      Sentence starters — use them if you want

      • In Act III, \"what's done is done\" means … , but in Act V, \"what's done cannot be undone\" means … , which shows ____.

      You don't have to use these.

      My answer

      Check my answer — after you've written yours

      In Act III it's a comfort: it's over, so forget it and move on. In Act V the same idea has turned into a trap: it's over, so she can never fix it or escape it. The words barely change; her relationship to the crime has completely flipped. If you saw the flip from 'let it go' to 'I can't let it go', you've got it.

      Performance words and claim words — open this any time
      Performance elementsEverything the script doesn't control: voice, body, light, set, sound, costume.
      VoiceLoud or soft, fast or slow, flat or shaking, where the pauses go.
      BodyHow the actor moves, stands, uses their hands and face.
      Light and setWhere the scene happens and how bright or dark it is.
      SoundMusic, silence, noises in the background.
      StagingOne director's version of a scene: all of those choices together.
      ClaimA sentence that argues something about the play, which someone could disagree with.
      Textual evidenceA quote from the play that backs the claim up, plus what it shows.

      Claim → quote → so it shows. Every argument today has those three links.
