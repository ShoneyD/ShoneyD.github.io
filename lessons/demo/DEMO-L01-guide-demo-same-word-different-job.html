      Introduce the Concept

      Part 1 — One word, three jobs

      A verb is the word that tells what the subject does or is. But here's the twist: the same word can do three different jobs. You can't label a verb by memory — you have to check what it's doing in this sentence.

      Two tests do all the work:

      • The = test. Swap the verb for =. Still makes sense? → linking. The soup tastes salty → soup = salty ✓
      • The alone test. Could this verb be the whole verb by itself? If it leans on another verb right after it → helping.

      If neither test fits, the subject is doing it → action.

      Part 2 — The whole verb phrase

      Helping verbs and the main verb travel together like a train: the helpers are the cars, the main verb is the engine. Find a verb phrase = find every car and the engine.

      Two traps:

      • Words like not, never, already, just can sit between the cars. They're not verbs.
      • In a question, the subject jumps into the middle: Did the coach see it?

      Part 2 — Where does it end?

      Every complete thought has its own subject + verb. When a new subject + verb starts, the old sentence has to end — or be joined with , and / , but / , so. A comma alone is not strong enough. That's a run-on.

      Verb jobs, the two tests, and the pronoun check — open this any time
      Action verbThe subject does something — even something you can't see (think, want, has).
      Linking verb — the = testSwap the verb for =. If the sentence still makes sense, it's linking: The soup tastes salty → soup = salty. ✓
      Helping verb — the alone testIt can't be the whole verb by itself; it leans on a main verb: has been studying. Helpers: am, is, are, was, were, be, been · has, have, had · do, does, did · can, could, will, would, shall, should, may, might, must.
      Verb phraseEvery helper plus the main verb. Words like not, never, already can sit in the middle — they are not part of it.
      Run-onTwo complete thoughts (subject + verb, subject + verb) glued with nothing, or only a comma. Fix: a period, or , and / , but / , so.
      Reflexive vs. intensive — the delete testDelete the -self word. Sentence breaks → reflexive (Ana hurt herself). Sentence still works → intensive, it's just emphasis (Ana herself built it).
      Demonstrativethis, that, these, those — pointing. Those are mine (pronoun) vs. those arrows (pointing at a noun).

      The question that does the work: what is the word doing in this sentence? Not what it usually does.
