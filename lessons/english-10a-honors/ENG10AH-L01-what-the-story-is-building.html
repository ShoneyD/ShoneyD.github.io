      Introduce the Concept

      Part 1 — The bricks and the building

      A story isn't just things happening. Every character, every argument, every event is a brick, and the bricks build toward a theme: an idea about life.

      Where we are in the book. Frank tells his own childhood, starting in Brooklyn. His father, Malachy, keeps losing jobs and spends wages on drink. His mother, Angela, tries to keep the children fed. After baby Margaret dies, the family gives up on America and goes back to Ireland, ending up poor in Limerick.

      Part 2 — Voices and word parts

      Dialogue without quotation marks. Open the book to any page with talking. McCourt never uses quotation marks. People's words run straight into Frank's memory of them, the way a kid remembers what grown-ups said.

      Q4. Why might McCourt leave out the quotation marks? Pick the one you think is strongest.

      My answer

      Check my answer — after you've written yours

      The first one is what most readers argue: no quotation marks make it feel like memory, not a transcript. If you picked something else and can give a reason from the book, that counts too.

      Taking a word apart. When a word is new, split it into pieces. The pieces give you a guess; the sentence around it checks the guess.

      Q5. Try one. Malnourished = mal- (bad) + nourish (feed) + -ed. What does it mean, and which part told you the most?

      Sentence starters — use them if you want

      • Malnourished means … . The part that told me the most was … ____.

      You don't have to use these.

      My answer

      Check my answer — after you've written yours

      Badly fed: not getting enough food, or the right food, to stay healthy. The root 'nourish' tells you it's about feeding; 'mal-' tells you it's going wrong.

      Q9. Unbearable = un- + bear + -able. What does it mean?

      My answer

      The context clue. Back in Ireland, Malachy has no work, so every week he stands in line for the dole, the small payment the government gives to men without jobs.

      Miserable: from the Latin root miser (wretched, unhappy) + -able. Unbearable: un- + bear + -able, too much to stand. Either works if you tie it to something that happens: hunger, Margaret's death, Malachy's drinking, the cold room in Limerick.

      The words for today — open this any time
      CharacterizationHow the writer shows what a person is like: what they say, what they do, how others react to them, and what the narrator notices.
      ConflictThe problem that pushes the story. Person vs. person, person vs. society (poverty, a whole system), or person vs. self.
      DialogueWhat characters say out loud. In Angela's Ashes there are no quotation marks; you hear voices the way a child remembers them.
      ThemeThe big idea about life the story keeps pointing at. A full sentence, not one word: 'Poverty…' is a topic; 'Poverty can…' is a theme.
      RootThe core of a word that carries its meaning. nourish = feed.
      Prefix / suffix (affixes)Pieces added to the front or back that change the meaning. mal- = bad. un- = not. -able = can be. -ed = it happened / it describes.
      Context clueWords around a new word that tell you what it means: a definition, an example, a contrast, or the situation itself.

      The chain: what a character says or does → what it shows about them → what the story is saying about life (theme).
